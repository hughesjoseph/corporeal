I have nothing to say, but I'm saying it anyway

About

A conversation that builds a space out of type. Each spoken sentence is set on a plane and hung in the room, the next one opposite, the next one further in, until talking has made a corridor.

The type is Corporeal, a 2013 screenshot of Helvetica: soft, with the orange and blue fringes of a screen's subpixels. Fly close and the letters gain depth, each layer further back blurrier and more dispersed, so you travel through gradients of the same colours.

Context

“I have nothing to say / and I am saying it.”
John Cage, Lecture on Nothing (c. 1949–50), published in Silence (1961)

Alan Fletcher carried the line into graphic design with his poster I Have Nothing to Say And I'm Saying It, and it returns in The Art of Looking Sideways (2001). The title bends it once more.

Corporeal's softness belongs to 90s editorial type: the blurred, layered pages of David Carson's Ray Gun.

Muriel Cooper and MIT's Visible Language Workshop hung text in space for readers to fly through in Information Landscapes (TED5, 1994). The Workshop's research also sits behind the anti-aliased type we read on screens.

Origin

In September 2013 I took screenshots of Helvetica on an iMac and enlarged them in Photoshop. The letters came back fringed: yellow and orange on the left of each stroke, blue and cyan on the right. This is subpixel rendering. Each pixel is a red, a green and a blue stripe, and the system lit them separately to smooth an edge.

I always imagined that accident as a kind of variable type in RGB, using the Z axis where the screen only had two dimensions. Separating and re-blending the channels in Photoshop never came close, because it isn't a blend. I called the result Corporeal. This sketch is the reconstruction.

Build notes

  1. Every glyph was cut from the 2013 specimen screenshot with its fringes intact, together with its baseline and original spacing. Sentences are set by laying those glyphs down like ink on white.
  2. The result is enlarged with a soft bicubic filter, like the screenshot in Photoshop: no pixels, only fringes.
  3. Up close, red and blue slide apart in the screen's own order (red right, blue left), so the fringes widen without leaving their palette.
  4. Depth is a soft volume, ray-marched in the browser: every layer of the letter is the same screenshot, blurrier and with wider fringes the deeper it sits, accumulated like smoke or a prism's shadow. No hard edges, only gradients.
  5. Speech comes through the browser's speech recognition; turns alternate walls. The demo is set with quotations from Cage, Cooper and Fletcher.

HTML, WebGL (three.js), Web Speech API.

Privacy

Nothing is recorded or stored. Speech recognition is provided by your browser, which may process audio on its own servers (Chrome uses Google). Best in Chrome on a laptop.
